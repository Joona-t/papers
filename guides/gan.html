Study guide · Neural networks · 2014

Generative Adversarial Networks

Ian Goodfellow et al. · NeurIPS 2014

How do you teach a network to produce realistic new images when you cannot write down a formula for "realistic"? Goodfellow and colleagues train two networks against each other: a generator that turns random noise into samples, and a discriminator that tries to tell those samples from real data. Each improves by exploiting the other's weaknesses, and in the ideal limit the generator's samples become indistinguishable from the real thing.

The paper in four sentences

A generator network takes random numbers and turns them into a sample, such as an image. A discriminator network looks at a sample and says how likely it is to be real rather than generated.

The discriminator is trained to catch fakes; the generator is trained, by backpropagating through the discriminator, to make samples the discriminator calls real.

The paper proves that if both networks were flexible enough and trained perfectly, this contest has exactly one end point: the generator produces the data distribution itself, and the discriminator can do no better than guessing, 50/50.

In practice the contest can be unstable, the generator can get stuck producing only a few kinds of output, and there is no easy way to measure how good the model is. Later work spent years fixing these problems.

Ideas in this guide

    Idea · §1–2

    The problem: making new data

    By 2014, neural networks were very good at recognising things: given a photo, say "cat". The reverse, generating a new, realistic photo of a cat, was much harder. The approaches of the time mostly tried to write down a probability for every possible image and then adjust it to fit the data. For rich models those probabilities involve sums over astronomically many possibilities, so the methods relied on slow approximations, often long chains of random sampling.

    This paper sidesteps all of that. It never writes down the probability of an image. It only needs a network that can produce samples, plus a way to tell it whether its samples are any good. The "way to tell it" is a second network.

    Everyday pictureThe paper's own analogy: the generator is a team of counterfeiters trying to print convincing banknotes, and the discriminator is the police trying to spot fakes. Competition drives both to improve until the fakes cannot be told from genuine notes.

    TakeawayInstead of writing down how probable each image is, train a sample-maker and judge its output with a learned critic.

    Idea · §3 · Fig. 1 · Algorithm 1

    Counterfeiter against detective

    Training alternates two moves. First, show the discriminator a handful of real examples and a handful of generated ones, and nudge it to score real ones near 1 and fakes near 0. Second, generate a fresh batch of fakes and nudge the generator so the discriminator scores them higher. The generator never sees real data directly; everything it learns comes through the discriminator's judgement, passed back by backpropagation.

    Below is a complete GAN in one dimension, small enough to watch. Real data are numbers clustered around 1.5 (the dashed bump). The generator takes a random number and stretches and shifts it, so it can learn where to put its output and how spread out to make it. The discriminator's opinion is the amber line: high where it thinks "real", low where it thinks "fake". This mirrors the paper's Fig. 1.

    TakeawayAlternate: train the detective on real versus fake, then train the counterfeiter to fool the current detective. The detective's verdict is the only teaching signal the generator gets.

    Check yourself
    How does the generator know which direction to change its output?
    It backpropagates through the discriminator: the slope of the discriminator's output with respect to the sample tells it which way would look "more real".
    Why does the amber line flatten out near 0.5 when training goes well?
    When fakes and real data overlap completely, no rule can tell them apart, so the best the discriminator can say anywhere is "50/50".

    Idea · §4.1 · Proposition 1, Theorem 1

    Where the contest ends

    The paper's theory asks: suppose the discriminator were perfect for whatever the generator is currently doing. What would it say? The answer is simple. At each possible sample, compare how common it is among real data with how common it is among fakes. The ideal discriminator outputs real ÷ (real + fake). Where only real data appear, it says 1; where only fakes appear, 0; where both are equally common, ½.

    Then they show that the generator's best response to such a discriminator is to make its distribution identical to the real data. At that point the detective is reduced to coin-flipping, and nothing either player can change will help it. That is the game's unique ideal end point.

    Drag the fake distribution around below and watch what the ideal detective would do.

    TakeawayThe perfect detective compares how common a sample is in real versus fake data; the only way to beat it is to make those two the same everywhere.

    Check yourself
    If fakes are twice as common as real data at some point, what does the ideal detective say there?
    real ÷ (real + fake) = 1 ÷ (1 + 2) = ⅓.

    Idea · §3, §6 · Table 2

    What can go wrong

    The proof assumes infinitely flexible networks and a discriminator retrained to perfection at every step. Real training takes small alternating steps, and the authors list the consequences honestly:

    • The pair must stay in step. If the generator trains too long against a stale discriminator, it can learn to send many different random inputs to the same output. The paper calls this the "Helvetica scenario"; it is now known as mode collapse: a face generator that only makes a few faces.
    • Weak early signal. At the start, fakes are obviously fake and the discriminator rejects them confidently, which in the original formulation gives the generator almost no gradient. The paper's fix is to train the generator to make the discriminator say "real" rather than to stop it saying "fake"; the end point is the same but the early push is much stronger.
    • No score. A GAN never computes the probability of an example, so it is hard to measure progress or compare models.

    TakeawayThe game has the right end point in theory, but in practice the two players can chase each other, collapse to a few outputs, or stall; and you cannot easily score the result.

    Check yourself
    What is mode collapse?
    The generator maps many different noise inputs to the same or very similar outputs, covering only part of the variety in the real data.

    Idea · §5, §7 · Fig. 2

    Small pictures, big afterlife

    The 2014 samples are small: handwritten digits, low-resolution faces and blurry CIFAR-10 objects (Fig. 2). The authors show the nearest real training image beside each sample, to demonstrate the network is not just copying. On a likelihood-style score (a rough one, they admit) the GAN was competitive with the best methods of the day.

    The conclusion sketches extensions that all happened: conditional GANs (generate a picture of a particular class), learned inference, and semi-supervised learning.

    Later refinementConvolutional GANs (DCGAN, 2015), the Wasserstein GAN (2017) for steadier training, and StyleGAN (2019) produced photorealistic faces. GANs led image generation for about five years until diffusion models overtook them on image quality and variety: "Diffusion Models Beat GANs" (2021). The adversarial idea lives on in many places, for example as an extra loss that sharpens the auto-encoders inside latent diffusion.

    TakeawayGANs showed that a learned critic can replace an explicit probability model; they dominated image synthesis until diffusion models.

    Abstract, restated

    The paper proposes estimating generative models via an adversarial process: simultaneously train a generative model G that captures the data distribution and a discriminative model D that estimates the probability that a sample came from the training data rather than from G. G is trained to maximise the probability of D making a mistake, a two-player minimax game. In the space of arbitrary functions a unique solution exists, with G recovering the data distribution and D equal to ½ everywhere. When G and D are multilayer perceptrons the whole system trains with backpropagation, with no Markov chains or unrolled approximate inference networks. Experiments show the potential via qualitative and quantitative evaluation of samples. NeurIPS (then NIPS) 2014.

    Sections

      Section · §3 · Eq. 1 · Algorithm 1

      The value function and training loop

      A prior pz(z) on noise, a differentiable generator G(z; θg) inducing a distribution pg on data space, and a discriminator D(x; θd) ∈ (0, 1) play

      minG maxD V(D, G) = Ex∼pdata[log D(x)] + Ez∼pz[log(1 − D(G(z)))] (1)

      D maximises the log-likelihood of a binary real-versus-fake classifier; G minimises it. Optimising D to completion in the inner loop is prohibitive and overfits on finite data, so the paper alternates:

      Algorithm 1 (minibatch SGD for GANs)
for number of training iterations:
    for k steps:
        sample m noise vectors z⁽¹⁾…z⁽ᵐ⁾ ∼ pz(z), m examples x⁽¹⁾…x⁽ᵐ⁾ ∼ pdata
        ascend   ∇θd (1/m) Σ [ log D(x⁽ⁱ⁾) + log(1 − D(G(z⁽ⁱ⁾))) ]
    sample m noise vectors
    descend  ∇θg (1/m) Σ log(1 − D(G(z⁽ⁱ⁾)))
k = 1 in the experiments (the least expensive option); momentum was used.

      The non-saturating generator loss. Early in training D(G(z)) ≈ 0 and log(1 − D) is flat there, so the generator's gradient vanishes. The paper instead trains G to maximise log D(G(z)), which has the same fixed point of the dynamics but much stronger gradients early on. Strictly, this changes the game from zero-sum minimax to a non-zero-sum one, so the theory of §4 applies to Eq. 1, not to what is usually trained.

      The figure trains the same one-dimensional GAN as on the Plain English tab: G(z) = az + b, z ∼ 𝒩(0, 1), and D(x) = σ(w1x + w2x² + c), which can represent the exact log density ratio between two Gaussians. Compare the two generator losses and different k.

      Check yourself
      Write the derivative of each generator loss with respect to D's logit ℓ (where D = σ(ℓ)).
      d/dℓ log(1 − σ(ℓ)) = −σ(ℓ) = −D, tiny when D ≈ 0. d/dℓ log σ(ℓ) = 1 − D, close to 1 when D ≈ 0. Hence the stronger early gradient.

      Section · §4 · Prop. 1, Thm. 1, Prop. 2 · Eqs. 2–6

      Theory: optimal discriminator and global optimum

      Working in function space (non-parametric limit). For fixed G, V = ∫ pdata(x) log D(x) + pg(x) log(1 − D(x)) dx, and y ↦ a log y + b log(1 − y) peaks at a/(a + b), so

      D*G(x) = pdata(x) / (pdata(x) + pg(x)) (2)

      Substituting gives the virtual training criterion C(G) = maxD V(G, D) (Eq. 4), and adding and subtracting log 4:

      C(G) = −log 4 + 2 · JSD(pdata ‖ pg) (6)

      Theorem 1: the global minimum of C(G) is achieved if and only if pg = pdata, where C(G) = −log 4 and D* = ½. Proposition 2: if G and D have enough capacity, D reaches its optimum given G at each step, and pg is updated to improve C(G), then pg converges to pdata; the argument uses convexity of V in pg. The paper flags that with an MLP the optimisation is over θg, not pg, so multiple critical points exist and the proof does not cover practice.

      Section · §5 · Table 1 · Figs. 2–3

      Experiments

      Datasets: MNIST, the Toronto Face Database (TFD) and CIFAR-10. Generators used a mixture of rectified linear and sigmoid activations; discriminators used maxout activations with dropout. Noise was fed only at the bottom layer of the generator, although the framework allows noise or dropout in intermediate layers too.

      Evaluation: fit a Gaussian Parzen window to samples from G and report the log-likelihood of test data under it, with the window width σ chosen by cross-validation. The authors note this estimator has fairly high variance and does not work well in high dimensions, but it was the best available.

      Section · Later work

      Limitations and lineage

      • Saturating divergence. When pdata and pg have nearly disjoint supports (common for images on low-dimensional manifolds), the JSD is constant at log 2 and an optimal D gives no useful gradient. Arjovsky and colleagues' Wasserstein GAN (2017) replaced it with the earth-mover distance; gradient penalties and spectral normalisation followed.
      • Non-convergent dynamics. Simultaneous gradient play can cycle rather than converge, as the toy above shows in miniature. Two-timescale learning rates, optimiser tweaks and regularisers became standard.
      • Mode collapse and evaluation. Inception Score (2016) and FID (2017) replaced Parzen windows; both reward diversity and realism without needing a likelihood.
      • Architecture. DCGAN (Radford et al., 2015) gave stable convolutional recipes; progressive growing and StyleGAN (Karras et al.) reached photorealism.
      Later refinementIn 2021 Dhariwal and Nichol showed diffusion models beating the best GANs on ImageNet sample quality, and diffusion (DDPM, latent diffusion) became the dominant image generator. GANs remain in use where one-step sampling speed matters, and adversarial losses survive as components (perceptual sharpening in auto-encoders, adversarial distillation of diffusion models). The VAE, published months earlier, is the main contemporary alternative: a likelihood bound with an inference network, at the cost of blurrier samples.

      Glossary

      Generator G
      Network mapping noise z to a sample; defines pg implicitly.
      Discriminator D
      Classifier giving the probability that x is real.
      Value function V
      Eq. 1; the binary log-likelihood D maximises and G minimises.
      D*G
      Optimal discriminator, pdata/(pdata + pg) (Eq. 2).
      JSD
      Jensen–Shannon divergence; symmetric, bounded by log 2; zero iff the distributions match.
      Non-saturating loss
      Generator maximises log D(G(z)) instead of minimising log(1 − D(G(z))).
      Mode collapse
      Generator covers only some modes of the data; the paper's "Helvetica scenario".
      Parzen window
      Kernel density estimate built from samples, used to approximate a likelihood.
