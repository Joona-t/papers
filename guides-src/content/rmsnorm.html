Layer normalisation does two things to each example's activations: it shifts them to average zero and scales them to a standard size. Zhang and Sennrich ask whether the shifting is needed at all. Their answer: mostly not. Rescaling by the root mean square alone matches layer norm's accuracy on a range of tasks while running noticeably faster, and this stripped-down normaliser is now standard in large language models.

The paper in four sentences

Layer normalisation takes the numbers a layer produces for one example, subtracts their average (re-centring) and divides by their spread (re-scaling). It stabilises training but adds noticeable computing time at every layer.

The authors guess that the re-scaling is what actually helps, and that the re-centring can be dropped.

RMSNorm divides the numbers by their root mean square (square them, average, take the square root) and then applies a learned gain. No average is subtracted.

Across translation, reading comprehension, image-caption matching and image classification, it performed about as well as layer norm and cut running time by between 7% and 64% depending on the model and implementation. It became the normaliser of choice in many large language models, including LLaMA.

Ideas in this guide

    Idea · §1, §3 · Fig. 1

    Layer norm works, but it costs time

    Deep networks train more smoothly when each layer's numbers are kept at a sensible size. Layer normalisation does this for one example at a time: it takes all the values a layer computes for that example, subtracts their average, divides by their spread (the standard deviation), and then applies a learned gain to each unit.

    The catch is speed. Computing an average and then a spread, for every example at every layer at every step, adds up, especially in recurrent networks where it happens at every time step. The paper's first figure shows a translation model where layer norm makes each step of training more productive, but the extra time per step eats into the overall gain when you measure progress against the clock.

    TakeawayLayer norm helps each training step but makes each step slower; the authors set out to keep the help and lose the cost.

    Idea · §4 · Eq. 4

    Keep the rescaling, drop the re-centring

    Layer norm makes the output immune to two kinds of change in a layer's numbers. If they all get bigger or smaller by the same factor, the output is unchanged (re-scaling invariance). If they all shift up or down by the same amount, the output is also unchanged (re-centring invariance).

    The authors' hypothesis is that only the first matters for stable training. So RMSNorm only rescales: it divides every value by the root mean square (RMS), a measure of typical size that does not subtract the average first. Square each value, take the average of the squares, take the square root. Then each unit applies its own learned gain, as before.

    Try it below. "Scale all" leaves both outputs exactly the same. "Shift all" changes RMSNorm's output but not layer norm's. When the values already average to zero, the two are identical.

    Everyday pictureAdjusting the volume of a recording. Layer norm removes any constant hum and then sets the volume; RMSNorm just sets the volume. If there was little hum to begin with, the result sounds the same and you skipped a step.

    TakeawayRMSNorm divides by the root mean square and keeps a learned gain. It keeps layer norm's insensitivity to scale but gives up insensitivity to a shared shift.

    When are layer norm and RMSNorm exactly the same?
    When the values already average to zero: then subtracting the mean does nothing and the standard deviation equals the RMS.

    Idea · §6 · Tables 2–10

    Same accuracy, less time

    The authors swapped RMSNorm in for layer norm across very different models: recurrent translation systems built in three software frameworks, a transformer translation model, a reading-comprehension model, an image-caption matching model, and a small image classifier. Quality was close to layer norm's in each case, sometimes a little better, sometimes a little worse. Running time dropped every time, though by very different amounts: the saving depends heavily on how much of the model's time is spent normalising and on how each framework implements it.

    Idea · Later work

    From a speed tweak to the LLM default

    RMSNorm's arrival was quiet. It became prominent when large language models adopted it: T5 (2019) used a layer norm with no mean subtraction and no bias, which is essentially RMSNorm, and Meta's LLaMA (2023) explicitly cites this paper and applies RMSNorm to the input of every transformer sub-layer. Many open models since have followed.

    At the scale of these models, every per-token operation is repeated trillions of times, so a cheaper normaliser with no loss in quality is an easy choice. Fewer parameters (no bias) and simpler numerics also help.

    Later refinementThe paper's "partial" variant, which estimates the RMS from only the first few percent of the values, did not catch on: the authors themselves found it was not consistently faster in practice. Modern hardware also narrows the gap, since fused kernels compute layer norm's mean and variance in one pass; RMSNorm stays popular mainly for its simplicity and equal quality.

    TakeawayDropping one step of layer norm cost nothing in quality, and the simpler normaliser became standard in LLMs.

    Abstract, restated

    Layer normalisation helps deep networks by stabilising training and speeding convergence through its re-centring and re-scaling invariances, but its computational overhead slows networks, notably RNNs. The paper hypothesises that re-centring invariance is dispensable and proposes root mean square layer normalisation (RMSNorm), which regularises the summed inputs to a neuron by their root mean square alone. RMSNorm is invariant to re-scaling and gives the model an implicit learning-rate adaptation, and is computationally simpler than LayerNorm. A partial variant, pRMSNorm, estimates the RMS from p% of the summed inputs. Across several tasks and architectures RMSNorm achieves performance comparable to LayerNorm while reducing running time by 7%–64%. NeurIPS 2019.

    Sections

      Section · §3–4 · Eqs. 1–4

      From LayerNorm to RMSNorm

      With summed inputs ai = Σj wijxj and yi = f(ai + bi) (Eq. 1), LayerNorm is

      āi = ((ai − μ) / σ) gi,   yi = f(āi + bi),   μ = (1/n) Σ ai,   σ = √((1/n) Σ (ai − μ)²) (2–3)

      with gain g initialised to 1. RMSNorm drops μ:

      āi = (ai / RMS(a)) gi,    RMS(a) = √( (1/n) Σi=1n ai2 ) (4)

      When μ = 0, RMSNorm equals LayerNorm. Since RMS(a)² = σ² + μ², RMSNorm divides by a larger quantity whenever the mean is non-zero, and the residual mean passes through scaled rather than removed. The normalised vector lies on a sphere of radius √n. The authors note that the plain L2 norm (which differs from RMS by the factor √n) did not work as a layer normaliser in their experiments (it appears as "L2-Norm" in Table 2), and suggest the √n scaling matters for robustness across layer sizes. In practice an ε is added inside the root, and modern implementations usually omit the bias b.

      Cost: LayerNorm needs the mean before it can compute the variance (two dependent reductions in a straightforward implementation, plus a subtraction per element); RMSNorm needs one reduction of squares.

      Check yourself
      For a with mean μ and standard deviation σ, by what factor does RMSNorm's output differ from LayerNorm's for a mean-zero component?
      LayerNorm divides by σ, RMSNorm by √(σ² + μ²), so mean-zero components are shrunk by σ/√(σ² + μ²) relative to LayerNorm, and the mean itself survives as μ/√(σ² + μ²).

      Section · §4.1–4.2 · Table 1 · Eqs. 5–10

      Invariance and gradient analysis

      RMS is linear in scale, RMS(αx) = αRMS(x) (Eq. 6), so scaling the weight matrix W → δW or the input x → δx leaves the output unchanged (Eq. 7). Unlike LayerNorm, RMSNorm is not invariant to re-centring. Table 1 of the paper extends LayerNorm's invariance table with RMSNorm; the figure recomputes it numerically.

      Gradients (§4.2). The gain gradient ∂ℒ/∂g is proportional to the normalised summed inputs, so it is invariant to scaling of x and W (Eq. 8). For the weights, the gradient (Eq. 9) involves a matrix R that scales as 1/δ when x or W is scaled by δ (Eq. 10). So ∂ℒ/∂W is invariant to input scaling and inversely proportional to weight scale: large weights get small gradients, an implicit learning-rate adaptation of the same kind that BatchNorm §3.3 noted. The authors argue this, not re-centring, is the source of normalisation's stabilising effect.

      Section · §5

      Partial RMSNorm

      If the summed inputs are roughly i.i.d., the RMS can be estimated from a subset. pRMSNorm uses the first k = ⌈n · p⌉ elements:

      RMS̄(a) = √( (1/k) Σi=1k ai2 )

      It shares RMSNorm's invariances. The paper uses p = 6.25% in its experiments (12.5% on CIFAR-10) and notes the estimate is biased and can make gradients unstable for small layers. In practice slicing overhead often cancelled the saving.

      Section · §7 and later work

      Limitations and lineage

      • Speed-ups are implementation-dependent. The 7%–64% range spans frameworks, hardware and architectures, timed on single runs; the transformer's gain was the smallest (6.9%). Fused kernels narrow the gap.
      • The hypothesis is empirical. The paper shows re-centring is dispensable on its tasks, not why in general. It also gives up an invariance, so a model with large shared offsets behaves differently under RMSNorm.
      • pRMSNorm was not consistently faster and can be unstable on small layers; it is rarely used.
      • Vision. On CIFAR-10, BatchNorm stayed better (Table 10).
      Later refinementThe archive's finding: drop layer norm's mean-centring and only rescale by the root mean square: similar accuracy for less compute, and now standard in LLMs such as LLaMA. LLaMA uses "pre-normalisation" with RMSNorm on the input of each attention and feed-forward sub-layer, following GPT-3-style pre-norm; T5's simplified layer norm is the same computation. RMSNorm is also used in places such as query-key normalisation inside attention in some recent models.

      Glossary

      RMS
      Root mean square: √(mean of squares). Equals the standard deviation when the mean is zero.
      Re-centring invariance
      Output unchanged when the same constant is added to all summed inputs.
      Re-scaling invariance
      Output unchanged when all summed inputs are multiplied by the same positive factor.
      Gain g
      Learned per-unit scale applied after normalisation.
      pRMSNorm
      RMSNorm estimated from the first p% of summed inputs.
      Pre-norm
      Normalising the input of each transformer sub-layer rather than its output.
      RNNSearch
      The attention-based recurrent translation model of Bahdanau et al.
