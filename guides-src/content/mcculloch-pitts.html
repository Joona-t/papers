A neurophysiologist and a teenage logician ask what a brain could compute if each nerve cell were a simple on/off switch. Their answer is the first artificial neuron, and a proof that networks of them can carry out any logical rule you can write down.

The paper in four sentences

A nerve cell either fires or it doesn't. McCulloch and Pitts treat that as a yes/no statement, like a light that is on or off.

A model neuron fires when enough of its "go" inputs are on at once and none of its "stop" inputs are. The number it needs is its threshold.

By wiring such neurons together you can build AND, OR and NOT, and from those any logical rule, including rules about time ("this happened, then that").

Loops of neurons can hold a signal, which gives the network memory. The weak spot: the wiring is fixed, so this neuron cannot learn. That came 15 years later with the perceptron.

Ideas in this guide

    Idea · Introduction

    A nerve cell is a yes-or-no switch

    In 1943 scientists already knew a key fact about nerve cells (neurons): a neuron does not fire "a little". Either the electrical pulse travels down it at full strength, or nothing happens. Biologists call this all-or-none.

    Warren McCulloch, a doctor studying the brain, and Walter Pitts, a self-taught logician who was still a teenager, noticed that "all-or-none" sounds a lot like "true or false". If a firing neuron means "true", then a brain is a machine that combines true/false statements. That is exactly what logic studies.

    So they asked a precise question: if neurons are switches, what can a network of them work out?

    Everyday pictureThink of a neuron as a doorbell that only rings if enough people press their buttons at the same moment, and nobody is holding the "do not disturb" switch.

    TakeawayBecause a neuron fires fully or not at all, its state can be read as a true/false statement, and the brain can be studied with logic.

    Check yourself
    What does "all-or-none" mean for a neuron?
    It either sends its full pulse or none at all. There is no half-strength firing.
    Why did that make logic a good tool?
    Logic deals with statements that are either true or false, which matches a neuron that is either firing or not.

    Idea · Assumptions

    The model neuron: count the votes

    Their neuron has two kinds of incoming wires. Excitatory wires vote "fire". Inhibitory wires are a veto. Each moment, the neuron counts how many excitatory wires are active. If the count reaches its threshold and no inhibitory wire is active, it fires in the next moment.

    That is the whole model. There are no shades of grey, no strengths: every "go" wire counts the same, and one veto beats any number of votes. Try it below.

    TakeawayA McCulloch–Pitts neuron fires when at least threshold excitatory inputs are on and no inhibitory input is on.

    Check yourself
    With threshold 2, inputs A and B on, and the veto on, does it fire?
    No. In this model a single active inhibitory input blocks firing completely.
    Do some excitatory inputs count more than others?
    No. In the 1943 model every excitatory input counts the same. Weighted inputs came with later models.

    Idea · Nets without circles

    Three settings give you AND, OR and NOT

    Change only the threshold and the same neuron behaves like different logic words. With two inputs and threshold 2, both must be on: that is AND. With threshold 1, either one is enough: that is OR. And a neuron that is kept firing by a steady input, unless the veto stops it, gives NOT.

    AND, OR and NOT are enough to build any logical rule at all. This is the same fact that makes computer chips possible: every chip is, at bottom, a huge pile of these three operations. McCulloch and Pitts showed neurons could do it in 1943, before the first general-purpose electronic computers were built.

    TakeawayOne kind of neuron, with different thresholds and vetoes, can act as AND, OR or NOT, which is enough to build any logic.

    Check yourself
    A neuron with two inputs fires when either is on. What is its threshold?
    1. That makes it an OR gate.
    Why is NOT the odd one out?
    It needs the veto. The neuron is kept firing by a steady input, and the input you want to negate is wired in as the inhibitory one.

    Idea · Expository figures

    Time matters: why a cold touch can feel hot

    Each neuron takes one tick of time to pass its signal on. So a network doesn't only combine signals; it also combines signals from different moments. That lets it detect patterns in time, like "cold for one tick, then nothing".

    The paper's favourite example is a real illusion: if something very cold touches your skin for just a moment and is removed, you can briefly feel heat. Their small network explains how. A "cold" feeling needs the cold signal to last two ticks in a row. A brief cold touch fails that test, and the same wiring routes it to the "hot" feeling instead.

    TakeawayBecause signals take time to travel, networks of simple neurons can recognise patterns over time, and this can explain real illusions.

    Check yourself
    Why does a brief cold touch produce "hot" here?
    "Cold" needs two ticks of cold in a row. A single tick fails that, and the rule for "hot" includes "cold at t−3 but not at t−2", which a single tick of cold satisfies.
    Why is the hot feeling delayed compared with the heat receptor?
    Every neuron in the chain adds one tick of delay.

    Idea · Nets with circles

    Loops give a network memory

    If you wire a neuron so that its output comes back to its own input, one pulse is enough to keep it firing tick after tick. The network now remembers that something happened, even after the input is gone.

    McCulloch and Pitts called these "nets with circles". They argued that with loops, plus something like an outside tape to read and write, such a network could compute anything Alan Turing's imaginary machine could compute. That is a big claim: in principle, a brain built from simple switches is as powerful as any computer.

    TakeawayLoops let a signal circulate, so the network can remember the past. With loops and outside storage, the authors argued, neurons could compute whatever a Turing machine can.

    Check yourself
    What does a self-loop remember?
    That a pulse arrived at some point in the past. It keeps firing until something inhibits it.
    Can this 1943 network learn from experience?
    No. Its wiring and thresholds are fixed. Learning from examples arrived with Rosenblatt's perceptron in 1958.

    Idea · Why it lasted

    What this paper started, and what it got wrong

    Real neurons turned out to be messier than on/off switches: their inputs have different strengths, their timing is not in neat ticks, and inhibition usually weakens firing instead of cancelling it. So this is not an accurate picture of the brain.

    But as an idea it was enormously productive. John von Neumann used these neurons to describe the logic of early computers. The mathematician Stephen Kleene studied what these networks can recognise, which led to "regular expressions", still used in every text search today. And the basic unit, "add up inputs, compare with a threshold, fire or not", is the ancestor of every artificial neuron in modern AI.

    Later refinementThe missing piece was learning. Rosenblatt's perceptron (1958) gave each input an adjustable strength (a weight) and a rule for changing it from examples.

    TakeawayThe 1943 neuron was too simple for biology but just right for computing: it is the starting point of artificial neural networks.

    Abstract, restated

    Assume neurons are all-or-none threshold elements with a fixed synaptic delay, absolute inhibition and invariant structure. Then the behaviour of any net can be described by a sentence of a temporal propositional logic, and conversely.

    For nets without circles (acyclic), the realisable behaviours are exactly the temporal propositional expressions (TPEs). Nets with circles additionally express quantification over the indefinite past, and the authors connect them to Turing computability.

    Sections: 1 Introduction · 2 The theory: nets without circles · 3 The theory: nets with circles · 4 Consequences · Expository figures. Bull. Math. Biophys. 5 (1943), 115–133.

    Sections

      Section · §1

      The five physical assumptions

      The model rests on five assumptions, stated explicitly in the introduction:

      1. The activity of the neuron is an all-or-none process.
      2. A certain fixed number of synapses must be excited within the period of latent addition to excite a neuron at any time, and this number is independent of previous activity and position on the neuron.
      3. The only significant delay within the nervous system is synaptic delay.
      4. The activity of any inhibitory synapse absolutely prevents excitation of the neuron at that time.
      5. The structure of the net does not change with time.

      Assumption 2 makes all excitatory synapses equally weighted and the threshold an integer count. Assumption 3 gives discrete time with the synaptic delay as the unit. Assumption 4 makes inhibition a veto, not a subtraction. Assumption 5 excludes learning by structural change, though the Introduction and Theorems V–VII of §2 argue that extinction, facilitation and learning can be imitated by fixed nets (Theorem VII: alterable synapses can be replaced by circles).

      Ni(t)  ≡  [ Σj ∈ Ei Nj(t−1) ≥ θi ]  ∧  ¬ ⋁k ∈ Ii Nk(t−1)(modern restatement)

      Here Ni(t) is the proposition "neuron i fires at time t", Ei and Ii are its excitatory and inhibitory afferents, and θi its threshold. The paper itself writes this with Carnap's Language II and a functor S for one-step delay, S P(t) ≡ P(t−1), in notation notoriously hard to read.

      Check yourself
      Which assumption rules out weighted synapses?
      Assumption 2: excitation needs a fixed number of synapses, independent of position on the neuron, so each excitatory synapse contributes equally.
      How does absolute inhibition differ from a negative weight?
      A negative weight subtracts from the sum and can be overcome by more excitation. Absolute inhibition forces the output to zero regardless of excitation.

      Section · §2

      Nets without circles compute exactly the TPEs

      Distinguish peripheral afferents (input neurons with no afferents inside the net) from the rest. A temporal propositional expression (TPE) is built from atomic propositions Nj(t) about peripheral afferents using ∨, ∧, ∧¬ and the delay operator S, applied finitely often. Each Ni of an acyclic net is equivalent to a TPE over the inputs at bounded earlier times, and conversely:

      • Theorem I (paraphrased): every net of order zero (no circles) can be solved in terms of TPEs.
      • Theorem II: every TPE is realisable by a net of order zero.

      One subtlety: expressions like ¬N1(t−1) alone are not realisable, because a neuron with no active input never fires. A realisable TPE must contain at least one un-negated atom to "carry" the excitation, which is why ∧¬ appears as the primitive instead of bare ¬. In practice NOT is built as A ∧ ¬B with A a steady source.

      Further theorems in §2 show equivalence between alternative assumptions: relative (subtractive) inhibition and extinction can be replaced by absolute inhibition (Theorems IV–V), facilitation by spatial summation (Theorem VI), and alterable synapses by circles (Theorem VII).

      Check yourself
      Why is ∧¬ primitive instead of ¬?
      A unit fires only if some excitatory input fires, so a purely negated expression has no excitation to carry it. Negation must be relative to a positive term.
      Can one MP unit compute x₁ XOR x₂?
      No. XOR is not a threshold function. It needs two layers, e.g. (x₁ ∧¬x₂) ∨ (x₂ ∧¬x₁).

      Section · Expository figures

      A worked net: the heat illusion

      Figure 1 of the paper includes a net for the illusion that a brief cold stimulus is felt as heat. With N1 the heat receptor, N2 the cold receptor, N3 "heat felt" and N4 "cold felt", the net realises:

      N3(t) :≡ N1(t−1) ∨ [ N2(t−3) ∧ ¬N2(t−2) ]
      N4(t) :≡ N2(t−2) ∧ N2(t−1)

      Two internal neurons implement the delays: one relays N2 for one step, and another computes N2(t−2) ∧ ¬N2(t−1) by inhibition and relays it to N3. The example shows the paper's real point: once the delay operator is available, a logical description of a net is also a description of a temporal pattern detector, and the net's behaviour can be "solved" by reading off the TPE. The interactive timeline on the Plain English tab simulates exactly these two equations.

      Check yourself
      What is the minimum depth (in synaptic delays) from N2 to N3?
      Three, which is why the brief-cold route reaches N3 later than the direct heat route (one delay).

      Section · §3

      Nets with circles, and Turing

      Once a net has cycles, a neuron's state can depend on inputs arbitrarily far in the past, so its behaviour needs expressions with quantifiers over past time ("there was some s < t such that…"). The authors characterise the class of such expressions and claim each is realisable by a net with circles. They then remark that such a net, if furnished with a tape, scanners and effectors, can compute exactly the numbers a Turing machine can.

      Section 3 is the weakest part of the paper. Its proofs are compressed and, as later readers found, contain errors. Stephen Kleene's "Representation of events in nerve nets and finite automata" (RAND memo 1951, published 1956) reworked it from scratch, showing that the events (input histories) a finite McCulloch–Pitts net can recognise are exactly what he called regular events, the origin of regular expressions and of the equivalence between such nets and finite automata. The Turing-completeness claim depends on the unbounded external tape; a finite net alone is a finite-state machine.

      Check yourself
      Why can't an acyclic net compute "x ever fired"?
      Its output at t depends only on inputs within a fixed window of depth equal to the longest path. Any finite window misses earlier events.
      Is a finite MP net Turing-complete on its own?
      No. It is a finite automaton. The Turing equivalence needs an unbounded external memory such as a tape.

      Section · §4 and legacy

      Consequences and later refinements

      In §4 the authors draw epistemological conclusions: because many different past input histories map to the same present state, knowledge of the world from the state of the net is necessarily incomplete, and "both the formal and the final aspects of that activity which we are wont to call mental are rigorously deduceable from present neurophysiology" (spelling as printed). They also argue that psychological phenomena like illusions, learning and even some psychiatric symptoms can in principle be described this way.

      What changed afterwards:

      • Computing. Von Neumann's First Draft of a Report on the EDVAC (1945) uses MP neurons as the notation for the logic of a stored-program computer.
      • Automata theory. Kleene (1956) cleaned up §3 into regular events; Minsky and others developed the equivalence with finite automata.
      • Learning. Hebb (1949) proposed synaptic change with use. Rosenblatt's perceptron (1958) introduced adjustable real-valued weights and a reinforcement procedure, dropping assumption 5. Modern units also drop assumptions 2 and 4: weights are real numbers and inhibition is a negative weight.
      • Biology. Real neurons show graded synaptic strengths, dendritic computation, continuous time and shunting inhibition; the MP model is now read as an abstraction, not a description.
      Modern formThe threshold unit survives as y = H(w·x − θ) with Heaviside step H. Replacing H with a differentiable function (sigmoid, later ReLU) made gradient training possible, see backpropagation (1986).

      Reference

      Glossary

      All-or-none
      A neuron's output is binary: a full spike or nothing.
      Threshold θ
      Number of simultaneously active excitatory inputs needed to fire.
      Absolute inhibition
      Any active inhibitory input prevents firing, whatever the excitation.
      Synaptic delay
      The unit of time; a neuron's output at t depends on inputs at t−1.
      Peripheral afferent
      An input neuron receiving no connections from within the net.
      TPE
      Temporal propositional expression: built from input atoms with ∨, ∧, ∧¬ and the delay S.
      Net with circles
      A net containing a directed cycle; gives memory of the indefinite past.
      Regular event
      Kleene's term for an input history recognisable by a finite net; the root of regular expressions.
